Glide typing lets you write a word by sliding one finger from letter to letter without lifting it. WM Keyboard watches the shape of the stroke rather than which keys it happens to cross, so a loose, fast swipe still lands the right word.

## Turning it on

<SettingsPath path="Typing / Gestures / Glide typing" />

Glide typing is on by default, and it isn't tied to one language. What it needs is a layout that types the letters you press rather than converting them, plus a word list covering enough of that layout's alphabet. English gets it on every layout registered under English, QWERTY or otherwise: AZERTY, Dvorak, Colemak, Workman and Halmak all glide, because the word list comes from the language and every one of those grids can spell it. Other languages glide as soon as their word list is installed and covers the keys. French, German, Spanish and the rest ship no built-in list, so until you download one under [Dictionaries](/languages/dictionaries/) a swipe on them has nothing to decode against and lands as taps. The keyboard says so rather than leaving you guessing: the first time you type in such a language with glide typing on, a chip on the suggestion strip reads "Glide typing needs a French word list". Tap it to open the language's page, where the list downloads, or ✕ to dismiss it. It shows once per language, for good rather than once each time the keyboard opens, and goes away as soon as you type.

Only languages that can actually gain a list from a download are ever asked. English and Bangla carry theirs inside the app, so they never raise the chip, and neither does a language you have set to [your own imported lists alone](/languages/dictionaries/#options), because a download would not be read while that is on. If glide typing does nothing on English or Bangla, the word list is switched off rather than missing: see [Dictionaries](/languages/dictionaries/).

A swipe only turns into a glide when several things line up at once:

- You're on the letters layer. Glide typing doesn't apply to the symbols or number pages.
- The swipe has somewhere to land. With no panel open that's your field. With a panel open it's that panel's own text box, if it has one: emoji and clipboard search, the dictionary lookup, a media panel's search bar (GIF, sticker, web, image, Wikipedia, Translate, App launcher, Vocabulary, QR), the AI prompt, Find and replace, the Learn-from-text speller, a plugin's text field, and the word card's spelling bar all take a swiped word at their own caret. See [Panels and captured keys](/tools/overview/#panels-and-captured-keys). The keypad boxes (the calculator's expression and the converters' amount) take none, since a word list means nothing on a keypad.
- The layout types letters rather than converting them. Hangul, Vietnamese and the CJK input methods all turn keystrokes into something other than the keys pressed, so a stroke over them spells a reading, not a word. The phonetic layouts are the exception: Avro for Bangla and [Hindi phonetic](/languages/hindi/). They convert too, but the grid is Latin and the output is another script, so a stroke is decoded against a romanized vocabulary rather than the native one: the language's known-spellings map, joined by its downloaded romanized word list (Banglish, Hinglish) if you have one. Without that spelling map there's nothing to read the stroke against, and the layout quietly stops gliding rather than guessing. Khipro is the other exception: its strokes are read against the Khipro spelling of every word in your Bangla lists, which the keyboard works out on the phone the first time and keeps. Khipro Dark glides on those same Classic spellings, so a stroke never has to cross the `/` key to join consonants. The fixed Bengali layouts, Probhat and Jatiya, shape clusters instead of converting, so a stroke over them is decoded against the Bengali word list on the ordinary path. Full [Pinyin](/languages/cjk/#swiping) and Japanese [Kana JIS](/languages/cjk/#swiping-on-kana-jis) glide too, in their own way: a stroke spells a reading, which joins the text being composed for the candidate strip to convert.
- The active word list covers enough of the layout's letters. Edit a layout in the [layout editor](/languages/custom-layouts/) and drop letters the language needs, and glide typing quietly turns itself off for that layout rather than guessing at a key that isn't there. It's worth checking first if swiping stops working after an edit. A finished dictionary download re-asks the same question, so a language can start gliding a minute after you add it. Only the language's own list is asked: a language you've added under **Also suggest from** doesn't count against the layout, so English with Polish mixed in still glides on QWERTY, and Polish words that need letters the grid lacks just aren't offered from a swipe. The same goes for lists you import under [Custom dictionaries](/reference/settings/dictionaries/#custom-dictionaries). While the language has a bundled or downloaded list, that list alone answers, so an English list kept under Arabic can't switch Arabic glide off, and a few imported Latin words can't switch it on for a grid that can't spell Arabic either. Imported words the grid can draw are swiped like any others. Your imports only get the say when they're all the language has: no list downloaded, or the language set to read its imported lists alone.
- You're not in the middle of a [typing-test tool](/tools/overview/) run, unless the test's own glide switch is on.
- The field allows it at all. Secure fields (password boxes) and structured keypads (email, number, phone, date/time) skip glide typing the same way they skip autocorrect: there's no dictionary word to guess on a PIN pad. A URL bar is the exception: it gets a swipe like a text field does, since a browser's address bar is typed as often as it's searched, even though autocorrect and learning still skip it.

The word list behind a glide is whatever dictionary is active for the language you're typing. That's the bundled one, or a bigger downloaded list if you've installed one under [Dictionaries](/languages/dictionaries/), since a larger downloaded list always takes over from the bundled copy. Words you've typed and had WM Keyboard learn are folded in too, and weighted heavily, so a word you've typed even once becomes an easy swipe target next time.

:::note
A letter-area swipe can do something other than glide a word: outside the F-Droid build <Flavor edition="google" /> you can set it to **handwrite** instead, drawing letters over the keys for on-device recognition. That's a separate feature with its own page: see [Handwriting](/tools/handwriting/#handwriting-without-opening-the-panel). Glide typing itself, the "Type words" mode, ships in every build.
:::

## The trail

While you swipe, a comet-style trail follows your finger: thick and opaque at the head, thinning and fading toward the tail, so the stroke dissolves behind you instead of leaving the whole path drawn on screen. It takes its color from your [theme](/themes/overview/)'s accent color by default, or from a color the theme sets specifically for it. Lifting your finger doesn't clear it instantly. The trail stays where it is and finishes fading on its own.

Three sliders under the same Gestures group tune it (trail width, how long it lingers, and its opacity). See [Options](#options) for their ranges and defaults.

:::tip
Power Saving mode drops the trail's opacity to zero to save battery while leaving glide typing itself working: see [Details & edge cases](#details--edge-cases).
:::

## Live preview while you swipe

You don't have to wait until you lift your finger to see what WM Keyboard thinks you're writing. Partway through a swipe, the suggestion strip already fills in with the current best guesses, and it updates as you keep moving. Those guesses are decoded against the same word list used for the final commit, so the word you see mid-swipe is never a preview of something different from what lands when you lift.

<PhoneFrame caption="Mid-swipe: the suggestion strip already shows the leading candidate before the finger lifts.">
  {/* shot: typing/glide-live-preview */}
</PhoneFrame>

Watching the candidates reshuffle under your finger isn't for everyone. **One word on the strip** (<SettingsPath path="Typing / Gestures" />, off by default) holds the strip to a single word for as long as the stroke is down: the same word the floating preview shows, and the same word a lift commits. The emoji, punctuation and rewrite chips left over from the word before the stroke stand down with the extra candidates, so the row carries one thing. Lift your finger and the alternates fill the strip as they always have, and the [ambiguity picker](#when-a-swipe-is-a-genuine-toss-up) still offers them mid-stroke if you hold still.

If you also use [words on the keys](/typing/words-on-the-keys/), that feature has its own answer for what a stroke does to the keys, under **While gliding**. It ships set to **Nothing**, so the keys stay bare for as long as your finger is down and the words come back when you lift. The other two choices put the decoder's alternatives on the keys so you can steer into one, or put the *next* word on the keys while you're still drawing this one, ready to flick the moment the swipe ends. See [During a glide](/typing/words-on-the-keys/#during-a-glide).

## Finishing a long word early

A swipe is normally read from end to end: the first letter goes where you started and the last where you stopped, so "dictionary" can't be offered until you have glided all ten letters. **Finish long words early** (<SettingsPath path="Typing / Gestures" />, off by default) lets the keyboard offer a word your stroke has only started, the way tapping four letters of it would. The guess shows in the floating preview and on the strip like any other reading, and lifting your finger types it. It's colored rather than only bolded when the keyboard is sure of it, under **Color the word a lift will type**.

Two rules keep it from running ahead of you:

- **A word you have drawn in full keeps its place.** Gliding "thing" all the way to the g reads "thing", not "things", and "that" stays "that" even when "that's" is a word you use a lot. A longer word only takes over when it's clearly the likelier one, letter for letter, over what you have actually spelled. "When sure" asks for a wide lead; "Eager" offers the longer word as soon as it's ahead at all.
- **Under "Prefer my words" and "Only my words", a guess is always one of your own words.** The dictionary still reads a stroke you finish, but it never finishes one for you, so a rare dictionary word can't jump in ahead of a word you have written a hundred times. Guessing the rest of a word is about twice as accurate out of one person's vocabulary as out of a dictionary, which is why the two settings belong together. Up to 0.5.11 the first rule stopped applying under "Prefer my words": a guess at one of your own words pushed the whole dictionary reading aside before the lead was measured, so "exam" came back "example" and "When sure" behaved exactly like "Eager". Your own words are still preferred, but only a word your stroke really spells can set that comparison now.

Lifting early on a guessed word is a choice, much like tapping a suggestion, so the strip treats it as one. Instead of the stroke's other readings (words that end where your finger stopped, which is nowhere useful), it fills with the word's neighbours: the plural, the possessive, the verb form, the shorter word the guess ran past. Lift on "dictionary" and "dictionary's" and "dictionaries" sit beside it; lift on "functionality" and "functional" is one tap away. Not available on phonetic layouts such as Avro and Hindi phonetic, where a stroke spells a romanization rather than the word itself.

A word you finish early teaches [your swipe style](#what-a-swipe-teaches) only what you actually drew. Lift on "dictionary" after "d-i-c-t" and the keyboard learns where your finger landed on those four keys, not on the six it never reached. No shape is kept for the word either: the shapes are of whole words, and yours was only the start of one. The word itself is still learned like any other.

## Alternates, and fixing a wrong guess

The moment you lift your finger, the best-scoring word commits to the field. The decoder hands back as many candidates as the suggestion strip has slots for, and the strip fills them from that list. The word that just committed is bolded in the center slot by default, following the same "best suggestion in the middle" layout that autocorrect and normal typing use, with its next-best rival to the left and a third option to the right. Tap any of them to swap it in for the word that just landed. Widen the strip (<SettingsPath path="Typing / Suggestions" />, **Number of suggestions**, three by default and up to six) and every extra slot gets a candidate too.

From the third slot on, the strip also keeps up to two words one key off the word that went in, the same slips a typed word keeps: glide "is" and "us" is offered too. A swipe is read from the key where your finger went down to the key where it lifted, so a start or finish one key over is the miss the decoder ranks lowest, and on a confident stroke its own readings used to fill every slot first. These words are only ever offered. A lift never types one, and they stay out of the [ambiguity picker](#when-a-swipe-is-a-genuine-toss-up)'s decision of whether a stroke is a close call. They aren't added under **Only my words**, after a word finished early, or on phonetic layouts. A swipe down or up on the strip right after a glide now pages through the stroke's own readings; up to 0.6.3 it showed the openers or next words left over from before the swipe.

<PhoneFrame caption="After the swipe lands, alternates sit either side of the committed word in the suggestion strip.">

</PhoneFrame>

Tap-to-replace only works while your cursor is still sitting exactly where the glide left it. Move the caret away, or type something else first, and the alternates for that word retire. The same guard governs a plain backspace: press it right after a glide and it deletes the whole swiped word in one go rather than a character at a time, since a wrong swipe shouldn't cost a letter-by-letter cleanup. That takes the trailing space with it, so one press puts the cursor back where the swipe found it. If your swipes tend to come out one or two letters off, turn off **Backspace removes a glided word** (see [Options](#options)) and backspace after a glide deletes one character at a time, the same as after a word typed on the keys.

## When a swipe is a genuine toss-up

Some strokes have no single right answer. On a fixed Bangla layout ক and খ share a key, so a word and its aspirated twin are drawn in exactly the same shape, and on any layout a hurried stroke can fit two words equally well. Rather than commit the likelier one and leave you to notice, **Ask when a glide is unclear** (on by default) offers the choice while your finger is still down.

When a stroke's best candidate barely leads its runner-up, keep your finger down and hold it still. After **Time to hold still** (350 ms by default) the closest words appear just above your fingertip, in their own window, so a stroke that ends on the top row floats them over the suggestion strip rather than hiding them under the keys. The keyboard's own first choice wears a thin outline, so you can see what a lift anywhere else will type. Slide onto a word and lift to type it. Lift without choosing and the keyboard commits its best guess exactly as it always did, and the same words are on the suggestion strip either way, so ignoring the picker costs nothing.

The moment the words appear, the stroke is finished. Sliding to a word does not redraw it, so the word you land on is the word that lands in the field. Change your mind about the whole swipe and slide down, more than a key below where you stopped: the words fade, a small "Lift to cancel" note appears, and lifting there types nothing at all. Slide back up and the words come back.

Four settings under the toggle tune it:

- **When to ask** decides how close a call a stroke has to be. *Near ties* asks only when the two words fit almost equally well. *Close calls*, the default, asks when the best word barely beats the next one. *Any doubt* asks whenever another word comes close. *Every pause* asks each time you hold still, whatever the keyboard thinks.
- **Time to hold still** is the pause before the words appear, from 150 ms to 1 s. Small movements do not count, so a finger resting on the glass still counts as still.
- **Hold longer to always ask** (on by default) lets a stroke the keyboard is sure of ask anyway: keep holding for twice the time and the words appear. Turn it off and a confident stroke never asks, however long you hold.
- **Words to choose from** sets how many words appear, from two to five. Up to three share a row above your finger; four or five add a second row above the first.

The picker appears only in the "Type words" swipe mode, since a handwritten stroke resolves through a different path. It works on a chained stroke too: pause on the last word of a swipe that crossed the spacebar and the choice is for that word. A word that is a snippet trigger or a personal dictionary shortcut shows what it expands to, like `omw = on my way`, since that is what lifting on it types.

## The space after a glided word

Lifting your finger commits the word *and* a space, so you can glide the next word or tap it out without the two running together. The space belongs to the keyboard rather than to you, which is what makes the next three things work:

- **Punctuation takes it back.** Type `.` `,` `?` `!` `;` `:` straight after a glide, or a closing `)` `]` `}` `"`, and the space steps aside, so you get "hello." and not "hello .". Opening brackets keep it, because "hello (world)" is what you meant.
- **A space press is spent on it.** Reaching for the spacebar out of habit doesn't leave a double gap. The press confirms the space that's already there. Press space *again* and you get the usual double-space full stop, exactly as if you'd typed the word out.
- **Backspace still undoes the whole thing.** The word and its space come out together.

A word glided back into the middle of a sentence, where a space already follows, doesn't get a second one, except in web pages, where the space is always typed ([why](/smart/autocorrect/#a-space-that-is-already-there)). A word swiped over a highlighted one is the exception to that exception: nothing on the page is a suggestion while a word is highlighted, so the space already after it is kept and no second one is added. The space is skipped in structured fields too: passwords, email addresses, web addresses and number pads, the same set that turns off every other typing courtesy.

Glide a word in front of a mark that closes up onto it, such as a full stop, a comma, a question mark or a closing bracket or quote, and the space waits. Put the cursor after "done" in "Is it done?" and glide `yet`, and you get "Is it done yet?", not "Is it done yet ?". The first letter you type there brings the space with it, and a mark, a space or a cursor move drops it.

A browser's address bar spaces a glided word when what you've typed reads as a search: a line that already has a space, or plain letters with no dot, slash or digit in them. Type `weather` and glide `today` and you get "weather today". After `example.` or `github.com/` the word still joins on, as an address needs.

For one word at a time, put **No space** somewhere you can reach it: a key, a flick or a long-press alternate in the [layout editor](/languages/custom-layouts/), a key of the [text editing panel](/tools/text-editing/), a [multi-finger swipe](/typing/gestures/) or a `?123` chord. Like Nintype's spaceless key, it takes back the space the keyboard just put after a glided or picked word, and the next word you glide joins straight on: glide `foot`, **No space**, glide `ball`, and you have "football". Tapped while a word is still being typed, two-thumb words included, it leaves the word open and the key lit: you can keep typing or swiping it, the strip still works, and when the keyboard ends it, with the all-thumbs-up pause or a pick from the strip, no space follows and autocorrect stays out of it. Press Space yourself and you get the space anyway. Tap again to call it off. A double tap ends the word on the spot, exactly as written. Right after a word and a space you typed yourself, it takes that space back too: type `foo`, Space, **No space**, glide `ball`, and you have "fooball". A space after a punctuation mark stays. Moving the cursor, Space, Enter or a backspace calls the joining off.

**No space** also works like Shift, for the one key after it. Double-tap it, or hold it, and the key lights up: the next key you type gets no space from the keyboard, before or after it. That is the `.` in `github.com` typed in an ordinary text field, where **Space after punctuation** would otherwise make it `github. com`. A space already in front of that key stays, so `best` then the armed `.` gives `best .tld` rather than `best. tld`. Holding the key only arms it: the word you are typing stays open and its suggestions stay on the strip. A word picked from the strip counts as the next key, so with the key lit, picking `specific` leaves no space after it and you can type on to `specifically`. Type the `.`, and the key goes dark again, so the next full stop is spaced as usual. Space, Enter, a new field or another tap of the key call it off, and a glided word counts as the next key, joining on with no space in front. On a flick, the arm does both: flick to **No space** and let go for the tap, or flick and hold your finger out on the arm until the key lights to arm it, so one flick up on the space bar carries the two. Flicking it twice quickly arms it as well. The arm on its own is also an action, **No space after the next key**, for a gesture or a chord, where there is no hold.

Turn this off with **Space after a glided word** (see [Options](#options)) and a glided word commits bare. Words chained in one stroke are still spaced from each other either way. The setting only governs the space after the last one.

## Gliding a whole phrase

You don't have to lift your finger between words. Start a glide, cross the spacebar without pausing, and keep gliding straight into the next word. Each crossing ends one word and starts the next, and the whole phrase lands together when you lift. A crossing gives a small haptic tick if haptics are on, and the floating preview shows the last few words of the phrase so far. An earlier word in it can change as you draw the ones after it. If you have the context model, it has its say on the lift, so a word can change once more there.

A crossing has to reach the middle of the spacebar. Dip past its midline and come back up into the next word. A stroke that only clips the top of the bar on its way to a bottom-row letter like <KeyCap>n</KeyCap> or <KeyCap>m</KeyCap> stays part of the word it was drawing, so a long word that hugs the bottom row isn't cut in two. Once you have crossed, the finger is on its way across until it climbs back to the middle of the bottom row: sliding off the end of the bar, running past its lower edge, or jittering along its top doesn't start a word or cross again.

The words either side of a crossing are read knowing where they end. A word drawn alone ends on its last letter, and the decoder leans on that. A word that runs on into the spacebar ends at the bar's edge instead, after a straight walk from its last letter, and the next word begins with the walk back up. The walk can be diagonal: the bar spans only the middle of the board, so a word ending on <KeyCap>p</KeyCap> heads for its near end. Those walks are read as walks, not as letters: the decoder may put the last letter anywhere on the way down and the first anywhere on the way up, so "I want to by a car" no longer comes out with "by" read off the key the finger passed on its way to the bar, and "the" followed by a walk no longer vanishes because no word ended there. This holds in the keyboard's own text boxes and the typing test too, and when another swipe engine is reading the stroke, the walks are cut off for it. Measured on 30,999 real swipes joined into the sentences they were drawn for, with walks that curve toward the bar's real extent, a word in a chain is read right 79% of the time, against 87% drawn alone. A word in a chain teaches the keyboard the word, but not where your finger goes for its letters: its ends are the bar, not the keys.

The stroke is read as a phrase, so each word is judged by the words after it as well as those before. "by a car" loses to "buy a car" once you have drawn "a car", and the first word takes the two already in the field into account. The evidence is what you have typed yourself, then the [word-pair list](/smart/suggestions/) for your language if you have downloaded it, then the context model if you have that too. Measured on sentences the strokes were not fitted on, with the English word-pair list, words read right go from 77% to 82%, two-word phrases from 65% to 73%, and four-word phrases from 41% to 52%. With the context model as well they reach 85%, 76% and 57%. Reading the same list one word at a time, as a chain used to, gets 81%, 71% and 49%. With neither download, only the words you have typed and a small built-in list of pairs can change a reading.

Only the final word of a multi-word glide gets alternates in the suggestion strip and the backspace-whole-word undo. The earlier words in the phrase have already committed and moved on. This behavior has its own toggle, **Glide across spacebar**: turn it off and a swipe that crosses the spacebar is read as a single word instead (see [Options](#options)).

## Typing with both thumbs

**Two-thumb typing** (<SettingsPath path="Typing / Gestures" />, off by default) lets both thumbs work on the same word at once, the way Nintype did. Each thumb can tap or swipe, at the same time as the other, and every tap and swipe adds to one word instead of each swipe being a word of its own.

Say you want "something". Tap <KeyCap>s</KeyCap> with your left thumb, swipe from <KeyCap>o</KeyCap> to <KeyCap>m</KeyCap> with your right, tap <KeyCap>e</KeyCap> with your left, then swipe <KeyCap>t</KeyCap> <KeyCap>h</KeyCap> <KeyCap>i</KeyCap> <KeyCap>n</KeyCap> <KeyCap>g</KeyCap> with your right. The thumbs can overlap: the next one can start before the last one has lifted. The keyboard keeps each thumb's letters in the order that thumb made them, works out how the two thumbs' letters fit together from when each one happened, and picks the word from the dictionary that fits best.

A tap that lands while the other thumb is still swiping goes into the word at that point of the swipe. Swipe over <KeyCap>h</KeyCap> <KeyCap>e</KeyCap> <KeyCap>l</KeyCap> <KeyCap>o</KeyCap> and tap <KeyCap>l</KeyCap> with the other thumb just after the swipe passes the l, and you get "hello". One tap can also stand for a doubled letter, so <KeyCap>k</KeyCap> <KeyCap>e</KeyCap> <KeyCap>p</KeyCap> tapped can give "keep".

A quick tap that rolls a little before you lift still types its key, as it does with one finger. **Delay after typing** works per thumb: right after a thumb taps, that thumb has to travel further before its stroke counts as a swipe, but the other thumb's taps never make it harder to start one. The keyboard tells the thumbs apart by the half of the board each one lands on.

A thumb can wait on a key while the other one types. If it stays put and the other thumb taps or swipes meanwhile, it's resting: holding it opens no popup and runs no hold action, and lifting it after 0.4 seconds or more types nothing. A quick overlap, one thumb landing just before the other lifts, still types both keys, and a long press while the other thumb is off the screen opens the popup as it always does. A resting thumb can still start a swipe from where it is. With **All thumbs up**, the pause before the word ends starts once only resting thumbs are left on the keys, so you don't have to lift them. A third finger that lands while one thumb is swiping is ignored.

A letter you gave flick arms in the layout editor keeps them. A tap types the letter, and a stroke counts as a flick for as long as it still could be one; once it travels past the flick reach or heads onto another letter where no arm points, it is a swipe, the same as with one finger. Keys whose arms repeat, such as a caret arm, keep the whole stroke for themselves.

[Swipe down to type the hint](/typing/gestures/#swiping-down-on-a-key-for-its-hint) and [Swipe up for a capital](/typing/gestures/#swiping-up-on-a-letter-for-its-capital) work for each thumb, by the same rules as with one finger. A short flick down on a letter types its corner hint and adds nothing to the word. A short flick up adds that letter as a capital to the word you're building, as a tap under a held Shift would, so you can start "Hello" with a flick and carry on with both thumbs.

The word is read again after every tap and swipe, so the word in the text field and the alternates on the suggestion strip change as you go. A word you are still building often isn't a word yet: the s and o-m of "something" spell nothing on their own. Then the field shows the start of a word they fit, here "som", or the letters under your gestures if nothing in the dictionary starts that way. Nothing you did is dropped. The next tap or swipe reads the whole word again, and tapping space types whatever the field shows, after reading the last gesture if it was still being read. A space, a mark or Enter pressed while that reading is still running waits for it, along with anything you type after it, so the word, then its space, then your next letters land in that order. Up to 0.6.1 the keyboard read the whole word again on the spot instead, which could hold up the key press for a long word. Backspace takes back your last tap or swipe, not the last letter, and the word is read again without it. When no swipe is left, what remains is the letters you tapped, as typed. That holds just after the word is typed too: swipe `car` and `pet`, let "carpet " go in, and the first Backspace takes out "pet" and the space, leaving "car" open for the next swipe. A word of a single swipe still comes out whole, with its space. And when a letter or swipe takes back the space **All thumbs up** typed, the word opens again as the taps and swipes it was, so a Backspace after that takes back a swipe rather than a letter.

**Finish a two-thumb word** decides what types the word:

- **Space** (the default). The word stays open while you tap and swipe. Tap space or punctuation to type it. Ordinary one-thumb glides work the same way in this mode: a swiped word waits for the space instead of getting one automatically.
- **All thumbs up.** The word is typed, with its space, once no thumb has touched the keyboard for a short pause, like ordinary glide typing with a moment's grace. A thumb that lands before the pause is over carries on the same word, so your thumbs can take turns on "movement" or put the second e of "heed" on the other thumb without having to overlap. Words you only tap end the same way: tap <KeyCap>T</KeyCap>, lift, tap <KeyCap>o</KeyCap>, and "To" is typed with its space once you stop, corrected the way a space would correct it. A word you have just backspaced into waits for your next letter instead. A finger on the suggestion strip or the toolbar counts as a thumb on the keys, so you can reach up for another reading without the first one being typed under your finger: the word stays open until you lift, the reading you tap replaces it, and a touch you drag off without picking starts the pause again. Up to 0.6.1 only the keys held the pause.

Four sliders and a switch, shown with All thumbs up, tune that pause:

- **Pause after a swiped word** is the pause for a word with a swipe in it, from 100 to 1500 ms, 350 ms by default. Raise it if words end before you have finished them, and lower it if you find yourself waiting for the space.
- **Pause after a tapped word** is the same pause for a word you only tapped. Tapping leaves longer gaps inside a word than swiping does, and stopping to think halfway through a tapped word is normal, so you may want more time here. It starts at the swiped-word pause.
- **Pause after a one-letter word** is the least a single letter waits, whichever pause it would otherwise get. A lone letter is far more often the start of a word than a word. It starts at the shorter of the two pauses above, so it changes nothing until you raise it.
- **Take the space back** handles a space that came too early. A letter you tap, or a swipe you start, within this time after the space takes it back and carries the word on, as long as the two still lead to a word in the dictionary. Type car, let the space land, then type pet, and you get "carpet". Type car, let the space land, then type dog, and it stays "car dog". If the letters stop leading anywhere partway, the space goes back where it was: car, then d, o, g reads "card" for a moment and splits back into "car dog". A letter typed after this time always starts a new word, and so does a capital. The time is counted to when your thumb lands, so a swipe you began in time still joins. It starts at twice the swiped-word pause, 700 ms by default, and goes up to 3 seconds. At the left end it is off. Twice the pause comes from the measurements published with [Kinetica](https://github.com/EZ-eta/kinetica), another two-thumb keyboard: about one change of word in eight came that soon after a space, and the dictionary check kept 70% of those apart. A full stop or comma after the space still takes the space and sits against the word ("car."), as before.
- **Show the pause on the strip** (on by default) draws a thin line along the bottom of the suggestion strip while your thumbs are up. It shrinks towards the middle over the pause, and the word is typed with its space when it is gone. A thumb that lands before then takes the line away and carries on the word. With reduced motion on, the line stays whole until the pause is over.

Up to 0.6.1 there was one pause for every word, nothing took the space back, and nothing showed the wait. These settings start where that one pause was, so the timing only changes once you move a slider. The tapped-word, one-letter and take-back rows follow the swiped-word pause until you move them; **Reset** on one of them makes it follow again.

When the next word's swipe joins a word because it began before the space, "car" and "pet" coming out "carpet", the strip offers the two apart: **car pet**, beside the joined word but never in its place, so Space still types "carpet". Tap it and "car" goes in with its space, and "pet" is the start of the next word, open for more taps and swipes. The chip stays on offer after the all-thumbs-up pause has typed the joined word, and picking it then takes that word and what it taught back out first. Undo the last swipe with Backspace and the strip offers it back as **↪ pet**, the start of the next word; anything else you type, swipe or move takes that chip away.

While a word is still being built, the floating word over the keys follows the stroke you are drawing, including the start of a word ("som") when the gestures so far finish none. A tap from one thumb while the other is still swiping joins the preview of that swipe instead of replacing it, and when the field shows only the start of a word, the suggestion strip offers the words it could become, as it does for typed letters.

Some glide features read one stroke as a whole word, so they are off while two-thumb typing is on: [gliding across the spacebar](#gliding-a-whole-phrase), [glide over Shift to capitalize](#capitalizing-without-leaving-the-stroke), [the picker for a toss-up](#when-a-swipe-is-a-genuine-toss-up), Hold to ask included. The settings screen's note on Two-thumb typing lists them too, and while two-thumb typing is on each of their rows says it is off. Their values stay as you set them for when you turn two-thumb typing off. [Pause, loop and wiggle marks](#marking-a-letter-as-you-glide) all count in a word that is one swipe and nothing else. In a swipe that is part of a bigger word only the loop counts: tap <KeyCap>m</KeyCap>, then swipe <KeyCap>e</KeyCap> <KeyCap>t</KeyCap> with a small circle on the <KeyCap>e</KeyCap>, and "meet" moves up the suggestions instead of trailing "net". Two-thumb typing works in the app you are typing in, and in the keyboard's own text boxes that have the keys: the searches, the AI prompt, a plugin's text field. There a swipe that lands right after letters joins them into one word, read again whole, and <KeyCap>Space</KeyCap> ends it; **All thumbs up** doesn't end a word in those boxes. The typing test and the word card's spelling bar keep one-finger glide. Phonetic layouts such as Avro, and keypads that guess, read every swipe as a word of its own.

A two-thumb word teaches the keyboard the way a glided word does. Each swipe in it teaches [your swipe style](#what-a-swipe-teaches) where your finger landed on the letters it drew, and a word that is one swipe and nothing else also files its shape. Backspacing the word straight after it is typed takes those lessons back. Fixing a two-thumb word later teaches only that you wanted the other word, never a typing mistake, since the decoder chose the letters and not your fingers. Letters the keyboard falls back to when your gestures finish no word, like "qy" for a swipe from <KeyCap>q</KeyCap> to <KeyCap>y</KeyCap>, are typed as shown but never counted towards learning a new word. A word you only tap is learned like any other typed word. Up to 0.6.1 two-thumb words were learned as if you had typed every letter.

[Statistics](/reference/settings/statistics/) counts a two-thumb word once, as a glided word, when it ends, and a word with more than one tap or swipe in it also goes towards the **Two-thumb typing** achievement. The keystrokes it saved are its letters less one for each tap and swipe.

### Finishing typed letters with a swipe

You can also do this with one thumb. **Swipe finishes typed letters** (<SettingsPath path="Typing / Gestures" />, off by default) makes a swipe right after typed letters finish them as one word: type <KeyCap>t</KeyCap> <KeyCap>o</KeyCap>, then swipe <KeyCap>g</KeyCap> <KeyCap>e</KeyCap> <KeyCap>t</KeyCap> <KeyCap>h</KeyCap> <KeyCap>e</KeyCap> <KeyCap>r</KeyCap>, and "together" is typed with a space, as a glided word is. With the setting off, the typed letters are a word of their own and the swipe types the next one. Two-thumb typing always works this way, so the setting is only shown while two-thumb typing is off.

A word you only put the caret back on doesn't count as typed letters. Tap at the end of "Hello" and swipe, and the swipe is a new word with its own space, with two-thumb typing on or off. Type or delete a letter in that word first and the swipe finishes it as usual. Up to 0.6.1 the swipe joined the word the caret landed on and came out glued to it.

## How it picks a word

The decoder scores every dictionary word that could plausibly match your stroke and returns the best one, weighing three things:

- **Shape.** How closely the drawn path matches the ideal path through that word's letters. Both are resampled to the same number of points, so a fast, loose swipe compares fairly against a careful one.
- **Start and end anchoring.** How close your stroke actually started and ended to that word's first and last letter keys. A word is thrown out early if the stroke doesn't begin and end near its endpoints at all.
- **Word frequency**, as a small tiebreaker. It's what makes "the" win out over similarly-shaped rivals like "they" or "them" when the early part of the stroke looks the same.

How much that tiebreaker counts depends on the language, not on you, so the keyboard sets it per language rather than offering a slider. The decoder's weights were fitted on English swipes, where the words people swipe are also the common ones in the list. German is different: its compounds are rare in any word list, so a frequency weight fitted on English lets a common short word outvote a well-drawn long one. Since 0.6.4 a German layout decodes with frequency at half its English weight and a stiffer charge for a detour between two keys. On 2,588 real German swipes that read 2.9 more in every hundred right, and 10.6 more on a corpus of rare words; the same weights would cost English about one in a hundred, which is why they apply to German alone. Everything you set under [Advanced tuning](#advanced-tuning) stays yours on every language; the per-language weights are the two no setting reaches. Other languages that build long compounds (Finnish, Dutch, Swedish, Norwegian) are the next candidates once their swipes are measured the same way.

A word list can't hold every compound a language builds as it goes, and German builds them constantly. On real German swipes, 36 in every 100 words of fifteen letters or more were missing from a 150,000-word list, and a bigger list wouldn't help: generating compounds into a list adds far more non-words than words. Since 0.6.4 a German layout reads a compound the list lacks from its parts. When a stroke has passed through a listed word of four letters or more and keeps going, the decoder may read what follows as a second listed word, or a third, on the same stroke, as if the list held the joined spelling. "Haushalt" is read from "Haus" and "Halt" when only the parts are listed. A word list says which words exist, not which pairs of them make a compound, so the joined spelling also has to appear in the full German frequency list, which the keyboard fetches once (5.5 MB, when **Download data automatically** is on and the connection isn't metered). Until it has, nothing is joined. That is what keeps "fragenroot" off the strip when you swipe "Garagentor". Every part needs four letters, so a short common word can't end a compound: "hast" and "alt" never make "hastalt". The joined reading ranks by its rarest part and pays a charge for each join, and a part the reading joins past pays more the commoner it is, so a word the list holds whole still wins over the same letters joined from two common ones. Without that last charge, two common parts outbid real words often enough to lose about 3.5 swipes in a hundred; with it, the same 2,588 German swipes read half a point better than with no joining at all. Linking letters between parts (the s in "Arbeitszeit") aren't read yet, so a compound built with one still needs to be in the list, or learned once you've typed it.

How forgiving those gates are is yours to set. **Start error radius**, **End error radius** and **Path error radius** live in the **Advanced tuning** fold (<SettingsPath path="Typing / Gestures" />) and are all measured in key widths. The first two are how far the ends of your stroke may sit from a word's first and last letter, and the third is how close the stroke has to pass a key on the way for that letter to still be available. They ship at 1.6×, 2.0× and 1.9×. Raise them when your swipes run wide or cut corners and the word you wanted keeps missing, and lower them to hold the keyboard to the keys you really crossed, which prunes the search a little as well. Going much under 1.3× is where words start dropping out, so move down in small steps and swipe a few sentences before you decide. They're also the quickest way to compare how a layout behaves under your thumb, since a fixed layout with crowded keys wants a different tolerance than a roomy one. Past 1.6×, the two end radii also change how a word scores. Whatever you add above 1.6× is distance the decoder forgives between where your finger went down (or lifted) and the word's first (or last) key, so a word that starts 2.5 keys from your touch-down competes on shape and frequency with one that starts right under it. That is the setting for thumbs that habitually land low or lift early. The end radius ships at 2.0× for that reason: on thousands of real recorded swipes, lifts stopped short of the last letter often enough that forgiving the last 0.4 of a key read more words right. At 1.6× or below, nothing is forgiven and the radii only decide which letters a word may start and end on.

None of those three looks at the word that came *before*. The stroke decoder is deliberately context-free: it reads the path and the candidate's own frequency, nothing else. Context arrives one step later. Where the language has a [context model](/smart/suggestions/) downloaded (English and romanized Bangla do), the model first nudges each word on the shortlist up or down by how much likelier it finds that word than the decoder's first choice after the words already in the field, within a fixed limit, so a close call between two strokes goes to the word that fits the sentence. Since 0.6.4 that counts for more on a swipe than on taps, because a stroke says less about its letters than taps do: on 2,294 German swipes with a word before them, measured with a simple word-pair model standing in for a real one, it took the decoder's first choice from 71 to 81 right in a hundred. The shortlist is then reordered by the same n-gram reranker the suggestion strip uses, and it's the top word *after* that reorder that commits. That step is **Context-aware suggestions** (<SettingsPath path="Typing / Automatic corrections" />, on by default and gated on autocorrect being on). Turn it off and the decoder's own order stands, so a glide commits purely on shape, anchoring and frequency. A word the model holds only in pieces, such as a long compound, gets a say too, and German weighs the sentence on its own scale.

Long words are read the same way as short ones, all the way to the 32-letter limit. The limit was 24 up to 0.6.4, which left a 26-letter compound like "Qualitätsmanagementsystem" unreadable however well it was drawn. A big word list holds thousands of short words that fit the first few keys of any stroke, so the search judges each partial word on how well it could still explain the stroke left ahead of it, not only on how well its own letters fit. Up to 0.6.0 it judged only the letters so far, and on a large list it ran out of room before reaching a long word at all: a German compound like "Computertomographie" or "Qualitätsmanagement" came back as a short word that fit a third of the stroke. On the 205,000-word German list, strokes of 15 to 17 letters went from about 1 in 7 read right to nearly all of them. A stroke that crosses the keyboard many times over is also read in finer steps than a short one, so the corners of a twenty-letter word don't blur together: drawn the way a finger really draws it, "internationalization" went from about 1 in 3 read right to 5 in 6. Since 0.6.4 the steps keep getting finer the longer the stroke runs, so a 30-letter word is read at about the same detail per letter as a 15-letter one rather than at half of it. Shorter words are read exactly as before.

A couple of shape quirks are worth knowing. Double letters only need one pass over the key, so "good" traces g-o-d, because drawing a letter twice in a row adds nothing a shape comparison can see. A small circle on the key says you meant the letter twice, and it has a section of its own below, with the two marks that say a letter is in the word at all. And a word needing a character with no key on your current layout, an accented letter your layout doesn't have, say, is quietly left out of the running rather than causing an error.

Every letter a key carries counts as that key for a swipe, not only the one it types: the letter on its Shift, the one in its corner and the ones behind its hold all sit where the key does, which is how a Bengali aspirate on Shift or a German `ü` behind `u` can be drawn. Since 0.6.4 that reaches keys that type no letter themselves. A digit on a custom number row with a letter behind its hold, or a punctuation key whose Shift is a letter, puts that letter on the grid at its own spot. It does so only for a letter no letter key already carries, so a letter key keeps its own letters and a digit holding the same letter adds nothing.

A stroke that strays above the letters is read as the top row it was reaching for. Going for `i`, a finger overshoots onto the `8` above it as easily as it dips into the spacebar below `n`, and nothing above the top row spells a word. Since 0.6.4 every point of a stroke above the top row is moved straight down onto that row's upper edge before the stroke is read, so drawing m, a, 8, n gives "main". The ends gained the most. A stroke that starts on the digit above its first letter used to start further from that letter than the [start error radius](#how-it-picks-a-word) allows, and gave nothing at all: twenty common words started that way on test strokes, a row and a fifth up, were read none of the time before and every time now. When a number-row key carries a letter of its own (above), the number row is part of the grid and the stroke is only moved down from above it. There is no setting for this, because a swipe can't type a digit: no stroke through the number row ever meant one.

Punctuation inside a word is the exception to that last rule. Hyphens, dots, at signs, underscores, ampersands, plus signs and slashes aren't on the keyboard's letter grid, so you can't draw them, and words are spelled with them all the time. Swipe the letters and leave the punctuation out: `fdroid` for "f-droid", `coop` for "co-op", `wasime` for "wasi.me". The word comes back punctuated the way your dictionary spells it. Where both spellings exist, the unpunctuated one goes first on the strip and the punctuated one sits right beside it, so "email" wins over "e-mail" on a tie and loses to it when your dictionary says "e-mail" is much the commoner of the two.

Some entries in a downloaded or imported word list are never offered for a swipe. The big English download is built from film subtitles, which spell out speech as people said it. That gives you cut-off words ("that-", "can-"), words with the full stop still on them ("no."), and stutters ("n-no", "c-c-can"). Each one draws the same as the real word, so all they could ever do was take the strip's other slots. A word list entry that ends on punctuation, or whose part before the hyphen starts the part after it again, is skipped. That rule also catches a handful of real words such as "D-Day" and "t-test". Words you added yourself or the keyboard learned from you are never skipped, however they're spelled.

The apostrophe is the one piece of punctuation left out of this, because contractions are exactly where guessing goes wrong. "its" against "it's", "were" against "we're" and "shed" against "she'd" are all one stroke each, and picking between them on word frequency alone would be a coin toss. It has its own answer instead: set **Apostrophe in a glide** to a key and swipe through that key. Without that key, the drawn word goes in and its contraction sits right behind it on the strip, with one exception: where the word before says a clause is starting, a pronoun's contraction goes in instead. Draw `hell` at the start of a sentence, or after words like *and*, *but*, *so*, *if*, *when*, *because*, *said* or *think*, and you get "he'll", with "hell" right behind it on the strip. The same goes for "she'll", "she'd", "we'd" and "I'd".

The contractions that are *not* ambiguous are swiped without it. Draw `doesnt`, `isnt`, `wasnt` or `shouldnt` and the keyboard reads the stroke as the contraction and writes "doesn't", the same repair **Fix missing apostrophes** makes when you type. This holds for every contraction in that repair's table, whether or not the word list has the bare spelling, and it goes away when that setting is off. Where the bare spelling is a rarer word of its own, such as `ill`, `cant` or `wont`, the stroke is weighed as the contraction it is far more often drawn for, so drawing `ill` gives "I'll".

Words you saved yourself with an apostrophe in them work the same way: save "Jenny's" and drawing `jennys` gives "Jenny's", no apostrophe key needed. That holds only where the spelling without the apostrophe is not a word in its own right.

## The FUTO Swipe engine

Everything above describes the keyboard's own decoder, which reads every language and layout. For English on a QWERTY layout there is a second one to choose from. **Glide engine** (<SettingsPath path="Typing / Gestures" />, **Built-in** by default) can be set to **FUTO Swipe**, which reads a swipe with two small neural networks published by FUTO: an encoder that reads the stroke and an English QWERTY decoder that turns it into letters, trained on about a million swipes people drew on real phones. The keyboard then spells words out of your own word lists from those letters, the same lists, added words and learned words the built-in decoder uses, and the result goes through the same context reranking, ambiguity picker and alternates on the strip.

On 5,000 swipes from FUTO's public test set (real people's English swipes, not drawn by a program), against the keyboard's bundled English list:

| | Right word first | Right word in the top four |
|---|---|---|
| Built-in decoder | 80% | 94% |
| FUTO Swipe | 96% | 99% |

Against the 160,000-word downloadable English list the built-in decoder scored 82% and 92%, FUTO Swipe 95% and 99%. Both figures count only swipes whose word is in the list, since neither engine can type a word it doesn't know. The built-in decoder stays the default until the same comparison has been made on phones.

Picking it asks you to accept the FUTO Model Weights License, then downloads the two models, 3.9 MB together, from FUTO's Hugging Face page, pinned to one version and checked against their SHA-256 checksums. On a build that leaves the ExecuTorch runtime out of the APK (the Play Store, GitHub and F-Droid builds fetch it on first use, the same runtime offline Asr4All, Parakeet and Moonshine run on), that comes down too. The models run on the phone, so your swipes never leave it. The licence asks that the keyboard say it is powered by FUTO Swipe, which the setting does under its row and the About screen does in its list of components.

FUTO Swipe only reads English on a QWERTY layout. Another language, another layout (AZERTY, QWERTZ, Dvorak, Colemak and the rest all move some letter to another row), a phonetic layout such as Avro, and any swipe before the models are on the phone all go to the built-in decoder, with nothing to switch. Two-thumb words are always read by the built-in decoder.

Three of the built-in decoder's features carry over to FUTO Swipe, each measured on FUTO's own test swipes before it was kept:

- **[Finishing long words early](#finishing-a-long-word-early).** Lift part way through a long word and the strip can offer the word you were spelling, the commonest one that starts with the letters you drew. On swipes cut at 70% of their length the right word came first 12 times in 100 instead of 7, and on fully drawn swipes a guess took the place of the word you drew 17 times in 38,000. FUTO Swipe scores words on a narrower scale than the built-in decoder, so **When sure** almost never offers a guess with it; use **Eager**.
- **A loop for a doubled letter.** FUTO Swipe reads "god" and "good" off the same keys, and a small circle on the o settles it for "good". The loop only decides between spellings that differ by doubling that key, so a curl drawn at a corner moves no other word. Without a loop the more common spelling leads, rather than the single one.
- **[Your swipe style](#what-a-swipe-teaches)'s shapes.** A word you have swiped before gets a little credit when you draw it the same way again. On words a person had already swiped earlier in their session, the right word came first 98.4 times in 100 instead of 98.1.

A pause or a wiggle on a key does not mark a letter with FUTO Swipe. The model reads the timing of your stroke for itself, and counting pauses on top of that made it less accurate on real swipes.

## Google's gesture library

The third choice under **Glide engine** is **Google's gesture library (imported)**. This is the closed-source glide engine from Google's own keyboard, the same file HeliBoard and other open keyboards based on AOSP can load. WM Keyboard doesn't include it, host it or download it, because it belongs to Google and isn't licensed for anyone else to hand out. You bring the file yourself.

The file is called `libjni_latinime.so`. GApps packages (the bundles of Google apps that custom Android builds install separately) carry it as `libjni_latinimegoogle.so`, usually inside a folder or zip called "swypelibs"; rename it or not, the keyboard accepts either name. There is one build for each processor type, and the keyboard takes only the exact builds HeliBoard accepts, checked by their SHA-256:

| Processor (ABI) | SHA-256 |
|---|---|
| arm64-v8a | `b1049983e6ac5cfc6d1c66e38959751044fad213dff0637a6cf1d2a2703e754f` |
| armeabi-v7a | `442a2a8bfcb25489564bc9433a916fa4dc0dba9000fe6f6f03f5939b985091e6` |
| x86_64 | `c882e12e6d48dd946e0b644c66868a720bd11ac3fecf152000e21a3d5abd59c9` |
| x86 | `bd946d126c957b5a6dea3bafa07fa36a27950b30e2b684dffc60746d0a1c7ad8` |

Almost every phone sold in the last several years is arm64-v8a, and the **Gesture library** row names the one your phone needs. Any other file is refused with a reason: a build for another processor says which one it is, and anything else is "not a build this keyboard knows". The check is strict on purpose. The library is machine code that runs inside the keyboard, written against one exact set of entry points, and a different build would crash it or behave in ways nobody has looked at. Turning off **Verify downloaded files** in Privacy doesn't relax it. Import a file only from a source you trust.

To set it up, pick **Google's gesture library (imported)** under **Glide engine**, tap **Import** on the **Gesture library** row and choose the file in the system file picker. The Storage screen's import (<SettingsPath path="About / Storage / Import downloaded files" />) also recognises it, by its name or by its checksum if it was renamed on the way; see [Importing instead of downloading](/privacy/offline/#importing-instead-of-downloading). So does opening the file from a file manager or a chat app: WM Keyboard asks **Import the gesture library?** and runs the same import. The file is copied into the keyboard's private storage, read-only, and it is never backed up. It loads the next time you swipe. Android can't unload a library, so replacing one that is already running takes effect after the keyboard restarts. **Remove gesture library** deletes the file and the dictionaries built for it, and glide typing goes back to the built-in decoder straight away. A library that was already loaded stays in memory until the keyboard restarts, because Android can't unload it, and the **Gesture library** row says so (**Removed**) instead of **Loaded**.

The library needs a dictionary in its own format. The keyboard builds one on the phone from the word lists of the language you're typing in, its commonest 120,000 words, and has the library write it so the format is always the library's own. That takes a few seconds the first time and is kept for later; a new or changed word list triggers a rebuild. Words you have added or the keyboard learned from you go into it as well. Until the dictionary is ready, and in a language with no word list, swipes are read by the built-in decoder as usual. The words it returns then go through the keyboard's own blocked-words list, context reranking, capitals and ambiguity picker, the same as with the other engines.

It works in any language whose layout has one letter per key; HeliBoard notes that keys with several letters or the same letter twice confuse it. [Finishing long words early](#finishing-a-long-word-early), the marks in the next section and the shapes [your swipe style](#what-a-swipe-teaches) keeps don't apply while it reads a swipe, and two-thumb words stay with the built-in decoder.

If the keyboard closes while the library is loading, building a dictionary or reading its first swipe, it is switched off and the row says so. **Try again** turns it back on. Nobody has yet measured how well it reads swipes on this keyboard, so the built-in decoder stays the default.

The Google Play version can't load it at all. Play doesn't allow an app to run code that didn't come from Play, so that build leaves the import out and the choice doesn't appear.

## Marking a letter as you glide

Some words are the same stroke. "good" and "god" are: the o is one key and your finger crosses it one time, so the shape of the path has nothing to say about which word you meant. So are "write", "wire" and "wrote", which all draw the same straight line along the top row of a QWERTY keyboard. Without a mark, the commoner word wins on frequency alone. There are two kinds of mark you can make on a key, and they mean different things:

- **Stay on the key: "this letter is in the word".** Slow to a stop on the i and carry on, and every word with no i there loses, so "wrote" drops away and "write" and "wire" are left. The stop counts for the key you stop on, not the keys next to it. The keyboard measures the pause against your own speed in that same stroke rather than in seconds, so it works the same whether you glide quickly or carefully. This one can't be switched off.
- **Rub back and forth on the key: the same thing, without stopping.** A wiggle is the moving way to make a pause, for when stopping feels slow. It's off out of the box, because a slow turn drawn with an unsteady finger looks much the same to the keyboard, and reading it marks keys you only went past. Turn it on if it's the mark you naturally make.
- **Draw a small circle on the key: "this letter, twice".** Swype's mark, and the only one that doubles a letter. Go around on the o once and carry on for "good". The circle costs the word nothing: the loop is taken back out of the distance your finger travelled, so "god" is no worse off for having been drawn with one. The circle belongs to the one key nearest its middle, so the keys its edge passes over stay out of the word, and a circle on the f of "off" can't turn into "odd". A circle on the last letter, with nothing after it but the lift, also says the word ends there, so with **Finish long words early** on a circle closing "see" stays "see" and isn't read as the start of a longer word.

A pause or a rub never doubles a letter. Without a circle, the two spellings tie on shape and the more common word leads: a plain "al" stroke reads as "all", "ben" as "been", and "ill" or "I'll" come up for a stroke from i to l. Only when the two are about as common does the single spelling keep a slight edge, and a circle on the key is how you settle that one. The circle also no longer draws in neighbouring letters, so a looped "need" stays "need" rather than turning into "needed", and "bee" doesn't become "bede". If you always pick the same word from the strip for a stroke, the keyboard learns that too.

Each has a row in the **Advanced tuning** fold (<SettingsPath path="Typing / Gestures" />). **Pause to mark a letter** sets how long a stop has to be. **Loop to double a letter** turns the circle on and off, and under it **Loop sensitivity** sets how much of a circle a curl has to be, **Biggest loop that counts** keeps a wide sweep through a ring of keys (the way "murderer" is drawn) from reading as a mark, and **Loop centring** sets how near the letter the middle of the circle has to sit. **Wiggle to mark a letter** does the same for the rub, with a strength and a size of its own.

These are worth moving because they're about your hand, not your layout: a careful glide pauses everywhere and a quick one barely pauses at all, and the same curl is a deliberate circle from one person and a shaky corner from another. If words lose letters you only went past, raise the pause; if your stops don't seem to count, lower it. If doubled letters keep appearing on their own, raise the loop sensitivity; if your circles don't count, lower it.

## Autocorrect and learning

### Going back to a misread swipe

Putting the cursor back on a swiped word puts that stroke's other readings on the strip, ahead of the strip's own answer. This matters more than it sounds. The strip normally reads the letters standing in the field, so a stroke misread as "form" offers "forms" and "fort": respellings of the wrong word, never the word you drew. The readings the stroke actually produced *do* contain it, so those go first, and taking one is both the fix and a lesson: it counts as a correction of that stroke, the same as re-picking straight after the swipe.

The readings are kept for the last 500 swiped words in the field and go when the field does. Each set is tied to the spot its word sits in, so the same word typed somewhere else in the text never picks up that stroke's readings.

Sometimes the readings don't contain it either. Under **Only my words** a swipe is answered out of the words the keyboard has learned from you, so a word it has never seen you write cannot come back however you draw it. That is the trade the setting makes. The stroke itself is still there, though, and it is the only record of what you meant: the letters left in the text are the keyboard's guess, not your finger's path. So hold any word on the suggestion strip while the cursor sits in the wrong word and pick **Search all words**. The stroke is read again against every word list you have, past the sandbox and past the vocabulary limit, and the strip fills with what the full dictionary makes of it. Take the right word and it both fixes the text and teaches the stroke: the hand model, the shape for that word and the pair, exactly as though you had picked it the moment you swiped. This is the proofreading pass a swipe otherwise doesn't get; tap typing has it for free, one letter at a time.

The search is asked for rather than automatic, because it is the slow, noisy decode that the vocabulary limit and **Only my words** exist to avoid, and running it on every word you read back would undo the setting. If you'd rather not go through the menu, **Offer a full search while proofreading** puts a small search button on the strip whenever the cursor lands in a swiped word whose stroke is still kept. Tap it to run the search; hold it to send it away. It is an icon rather than a sentence on purpose: the words beside it are the ones the search is offering to improve on, and a chip that spelled the offer out took half a phone's strip with it. It's still off until you turn it on.

Paths are much heavier than readings, so by default only the newest 24 swiped words in the field keep theirs. Past that the readings remain and the full search is no longer offered. **Swiped words a full search can reach** (<SettingsPath path="Typing / Gestures" />, beside **Offer a full search while proofreading**) changes that number, from 0 to 500. Each path is a few kilobytes, so 500 can take a few megabytes while you write a long text; 0 keeps none, and the full search is never offered.

Autocorrect never runs a second pass over a glided word. The decoder only ever returns real dictionary words in the first place, so there's no typo to fix. What glide typing does share with normal typing is learning: every word a glide commits goes through the same learning path a tapped-out word does. It's reinforced in your personal dictionary and recorded as following whatever word came before, which is what powers next-word prediction later. It works the other direction too. A word you've recently typed or had autocorrected becomes swipeable right away, since learning a word immediately refreshes the list glide typing decodes against.

Learning from a glide follows the same rules as any other typing. It respects the "Learn from typing" toggle, pauses under incognito, and skips entirely in fields that don't allow typing intelligence. There's no glide-specific exception.

### What a swipe teaches

A kept swipe teaches three more things besides the word, all under **Learn my swipe style**. The first is where your finger actually lands. Nobody swipes through the exact middle of every key: a thumb cuts the far keys short and lands low on the near ones, and every swipe used to pay for that. Each swipe you keep records where your finger really passed each letter, and later swipes are read against keys moved to match, so a consistent miss stops costing anything, and so does a hand that draws every word a little smaller than the keyboard and never quite reaches the far keys. The second is how you draw each word. The decoder compares a stroke against the textbook path through a word's keys, and nobody draws that either: you cut the same corners the same way every time. So a few of your own shapes are kept per word (three, unless you change **Shapes kept per word** under [Advanced tuning](#advanced-tuning)), and a word you have swiped before is read against them as well as the textbook path. The shape only lands once the word has settled as a learned word, never at the moment you swipe it, so a swipe you take back or replace teaches nothing, and one you replace marks the shape that read it wrongly. The third is what you do about a wrong reading. Take a different word off the strip, lift on a picker word that wasn't the leader, or backspace a swiped word straight away, and the keyboard remembers it against that pair of words. It is a nudge, not a rule: three consistent corrections are enough to flip a close call, and the same stroke then reads your word first without asking, but no habit overturns a reading the stroke makes clearly, and a correction the other way cancels the first. Backspacing a swiped word takes its lessons back. If you then swipe or type the word you meant within ten seconds, the stroke teaches that word instead, but only when it started and ended within about two keys of that word's first and last letters. Delete a swiped "the" and type "a" and nothing is learned, since that is an edit of the sentence rather than another go at the swipe. Up to 0.6.2 any word typed next took the stroke.

Only the shapes are filed under words, and only under words already in your personal dictionary. The finger positions are numbers on a grid, and the corrected readings are scrambled fingerprints of the words, so those files say nothing readable about what you wrote. All three travel in a backup under **Swipe style**, which is off until you turn it on, for the same reason the dictionary is: the shapes are filed under the words you drew them for. **Forget my swipe style** deletes all three and starts over, and leaves your learned words alone; the word card a held suggestion opens can also forget the shapes of one word.

Your hand is allowed to change its mind. A word you start drawing from a different place, or hold at a different angle, is a new shape: keep drawing it the new way and the new shape settles in and the old one fades out, rather than the first way you ever drew it holding the slot for good.

It also no longer matters how you make the correction. Taking the right word off the strip, backspacing the wrong word and picking off the strip that comes back, and backspacing it and drawing again all teach the keyboard the same three things. Backspacing first used to teach nothing at all: the stroke was thrown away the moment the word came off, so there was nothing left to attach the right answer to, and the same correction could be made over and over without the reading ever moving. Now the stroke waits a few seconds for whatever you put in its place.

One more shift-related detail: only the first word of a multi-word glide honors a held <KeyCap>Shift</KeyCap>, capitalizing it or, under caps lock, the whole word. Words chained afterward in the same stroke always land lowercase.

### Capitalizing without leaving the stroke

Tapping <KeyCap>Shift</KeyCap> and *then* swiping is two gestures for one word, and it breaks the flow that makes gliding worth using. That's why most people stop capitalizing swiped names. So the stroke can say it itself: draw through the Shift key on your way and the word comes out capitalized. Cross it twice in one stroke and the whole word is in capitals, the same ladder tapping the key walks up.

The detour spells nothing. The walk out to the Shift key and the walk back are cut from the path before the word is read, not only the key itself, so going out to the key and back never adds a letter: a stroke drawn t→Shift→h→e→r→e reads as "there", not as whatever lies between `t` and the key. Nothing happens unless the key is actually crossed, so ordinary swipes near it are unaffected, and the switch (**Glide over Shift to capitalize**) turns it off entirely. The preview keeps up: from the moment the stroke crosses the key, the word floating above your finger and the candidates on the strip show the capital the lift will type.

**What a pass over Shift capitalizes** chooses what a crossing means. *Whole word* is the ladder above. *One letter* reads each crossing as a capital for the letter your finger just left, so a stroke can write `HeLLo` or `LeanType`: glide to the letter, out to Shift, and on to the next letter, as many times as the word needs. Under that reading a whole-word shout is one trip rather than two: end the stroke on the Shift key and every letter is a capital. A capital the keyboard armed itself at a sentence start still stands, so a drawn capital and an automatic one can be different letters of the same word.

This is also the one way a swipe can teach a capital. A capital the *keyboard* chose is never evidence of how you spell a word (see [Personal dictionary](/smart/personal-dictionary/#capitals)); a capital you drew is.

## The glide check-up

Two of the numbers behind a swipe depend on what you write, not on the keyboard: how much of the dictionary a swipe may answer with (**Words a glide can produce**) and how much a word's commonness counts against the shape you drew. The second has no slider. It is set per language, because a word list's frequencies mean different things in different languages, and the right value also moves with the person: someone who writes rare words wants shape to count for more than someone whose words are the common ones. The check-up (<SettingsPath path="Typing / Gestures / Glide typing / Glide check-up" />) finds both from your own swipes instead of asking you to guess. It is off until you open it and turn **Measure my swipes** on.

While it measures, each word you swipe is read again in the background under a few candidate values, and only the words that come out are kept, never the stroke. If you later fix that word, by taking another reading off the strip, running a full search, or backspacing it and drawing again, the record is updated with the word you meant. After 30 swipes the screen proposes one change at a time, and only one that would have read more of your own swipes right. It says so in those terms: "On your last 60 swipes this would have read 56 right instead of 52: 5 more, 1 fewer." The proposal is judged on swipes the fit never saw, so a value that happens to suit half your words and not the other half is not offered. The vocabulary limit is tried first, then the frequency weight; the radii and the other geometry are not on the ladder, because on thousands of real swipes they were already at their best and a per-person fit had nothing to win.

**Apply** sets the value, **Not now** puts that candidate aside until you start over, and **Undo** puts the old value back. A proposal measured against a value you have since changed by hand is refused rather than applied over it, and measuring starts again from the new value. After you apply a change the check-up watches the next 40 swipes and compares how often you corrected a swiped word before and after. It then says which way it went and offers **Keep** or **Undo**; nothing is ever undone for you. The screen also shows what it learned about your hand, in key widths: how far inside a corner you cut, where your finger sits relative to the letters, how much of a word's span a stroke covers, and how far from the first and last letters it lands and lifts. A fitted frequency weight is listed at the bottom of the screen with a reset back to the language's own; **Start over** forgets everything measured.

## Details & edge cases

**Power Saving mode splits glide typing into two independent switches.** By default, power saving drops only the trail: it forces trail opacity to zero, so nothing is drawn while you swipe, and decoding and committing carry on as normal. Turning glide typing off altogether under power saving is a separate option, and it's off by default. Decoding a swipe really is the most expensive thing the keyboard does, but it's also why many people installed a gesture keyboard, so the cheap fix ships on and the expensive one is opt-in. See [Power saving](/typing/power-saving/).

**A layout that can't spell the language turns glide typing off rather than breaking it.** The decoder throws out any word holding a character the grid can't produce, so a layout covering only a fraction of a language would never fail loudly. It would decode against that fraction and commit confident nonsense. Instead, WM Keyboard takes the language's most common words, counts how many the layout can draw end to end, and wants at least 90% of them before it will glide. The threshold sits short of 100% on purpose: real word lists carry a few loanwords and proper nouns in other scripts, and demanding every one of them would fail layouts that handle everything people actually type. Words spelled with an apostrophe or a hyphen the layout has no key for, such as French `c'est`, `d'un` and `lui-même`, aren't counted either way, since no layout can draw them. Up to 0.6.0 they counted as misses, and in French they are about one common word in eleven, so a layout carrying é, è, ê, à, ù and ç on its keys but not â, î and ô (an imported Unexpected Keyboard AZERTY, say) scored just under 90% and lost glide typing entirely. The common words come from the language's own bundled or downloaded list. Up to 0.5.10 an imported list was counted alongside it with an equal share, so one import the grid couldn't spell (an English list under Arabic, or a compiled `.dict` that 0.5.9 had copied in without unpacking it) halved the score and every swipe landed as taps, with nothing on screen to say why. Imports no longer count while the language has a list of its own, and those old `.dict` copies are [repaired automatically](/reference/settings/dictionaries/#custom-dictionaries).

**Multi-word glides only leave the last word revertible.** If a phrase-length glide gets one word wrong, tap-to-fix and the backspace-whole-word shortcut apply only to the final word in the stroke. The earlier words are ordinary committed text by then.

**The bigram context updates correctly when you swap an alternate.** Tap an alternate to replace a glide's word and the "what word came before" tracking re-links to the word before the one you just replaced, rather than to the discarded guess. Next-word prediction stays accurate after a correction.
