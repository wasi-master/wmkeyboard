Bangla is one of only two languages that ship a dictionary and emoji keywords inside the app itself. English is the other, and Bangla is the only one of the two with a purpose-built phonetic engine on top. It ships six layouts: the phonetic Avro, and five fixed ones, Probhat, Jatiya, Baishakhi, Bornona, and InScript. Adding Bangla turns on Avro, and the rest are a tap away on its own screen. A phone whose Android language list already includes Bangla gets it enabled from the first run. Type romanized Bangla and the keyboard converts it to script as you go, or pick one of the fixed keyboards if you'd rather type the script directly.

## Typing phonetically with Avro

**Avro phonetic** is a fixed QWERTY grid underneath (every key is exactly where it is on the English layout) with a rule-based transliteration engine layered on top. Type a romanized syllable and it renders in Bengali script directly in the composing text, no separate conversion step: "ami" becomes আমি, "kolokata" becomes কলকাতা, "dhorrmo" becomes ধর্ম. The engine handles conjuncts, reph, khanda-ta, and the vowel/consonant context that decides whether a vowel is a standalone letter or a kar sign. All of that is automatic, so you type the sounds and leave the script mechanics alone.

Where it goes further than a plain transliterator is lenient spelling. Bangla speakers write the same word several different ways in Latin letters (আছি gets typed "achi", "achhi" or "asi" depending on the person), and Avro takes them all. Two layers do that, in this order.

First a hand-built list of about 12,000 romanized spellings, described under [Spellings the rules can't reach](#spellings-the-rules-cant-reach) below. It covers most of the everyday vocabulary, and a spelling it names is settled there and nowhere else, because the list outranks both the dictionary and the literal transliteration. Both `achi` and `asi` are on it, and they're listed separately: `achi` commits আছি, `asi` commits আসি.

Anything the list has never heard of falls to a phonetic fold. Both your romanized input and every word in the Bangla dictionary are reduced to a lenient key where confusable sounds collapse (স/শ/ষ/ছ/চ all fold to the same sound, aspiration like "kh" or "bh" drops to "k"/"b", and so on), so an unlisted spelling still finds the dictionary word it sounds like. Words sharing a key are phonetic siblings, and the most frequent sibling leads. Two things you wrote down are weighed before frequency, because the fold throws them away: a sibling that contradicts an "h" you actually typed is pushed down, and so is one that ends in ও when you typed no final o. The "ch" of চ and the "ph" of ফ are exempt, since those are ordinary English-influenced spellings rather than claims about aspiration.

A sibling only takes the commit from the literal transliteration when it is at least twice as frequent. A near-tie leaves what the composing text already showed you, because a silent swap between two words of roughly equal currency reads as a bug rather than a correction. The exception is a literal that isn't a dictionary word at all: with nothing to protect, the siblings lead. The literal transliteration is always in the suggestion strip as a fallback either way, so you can pick it if the substitution guesses wrong. To stop the substitution altogether, turn off **Correct to dictionary words** (see [Options](#options)).

:::note
Neither layer is part of autocorrect, and neither is controlled by the autocorrect toggle. Avro's phonetic commit runs regardless of whether [Autocorrect](/smart/autocorrect/) is on or off. The fixed-spelling list has its own switch (see **Spellings** under [Options](#options) below). The phonetic fold has none.
:::

A few more of the rules worth knowing:

| You type | You get | Why |
|---|---|---|
| `valo` or `bhalo` | ভাল | "bh" and "v" both fold to the "b" sound |
| `valO` | ভালো | lowercase "o" is the inherent vowel and writes nothing; only capital "O" is ও/ো |
| `kolokata` | কলকাতা | the explicit "o" between l and k breaks the conjunct that `kolkata` (no vowel between them) would form |
| `bangla` / `bangali` | বাংলা / বাঙালি | ং joins nothing; before a vowel it is ঙ, which can carry the syllable |
| `swasthyo` | স্বাস্থ্য | "w" after a consonant is a ব-ফলা join |
| `wasi` | ওয়াসি | "w" anywhere else is ও |
| `panjabi` | পাঞ্জাবি | "n" before "j" is ঞ |
| `hoThaTH`, `hoThat` + a trailing backquote, or `hoThat,,` | হঠাৎ | `TH`, a backquote or a trailing hasant all spell khanda-ta (ৎ) |
| `shyam` | শ্যাম | "y" after a consonant is a য-ফলা join |
| `meye` | মেয়ে | "y" elsewhere is য়, not a join |
| `ggan` | জ্ঞান | `gg` is the জ্ঞ conjunct |
| `Bangla` | বাংলা | a capital that spells nothing of its own reads as its lowercase self |
| `$` / `123` | ৳ / ১২৩ | currency and digits convert too |
| `.` / `..` | । / `.` | a single dot is দাঁড়ি; two dots is a literal period |

Both of Avro's spellings for the marks work, and so do the ones Ridmik uses: ঁ is `^` or `qq`/`cb`, ঃ is `:` or `HH`, ৎ is `t` plus a backquote or `TH`, and the hasant is `,,` or `hs`. The `hs` form only fires after a consonant, where a hasant can actually go, so it never eats the h and s of a name like `ahsan`.

**Vowels.** The first form is the letter on its own (at the start of a word, or after another vowel). The second is the sign it becomes after a consonant.

|---|---|---|

### Spellings the rules can't reach

Some words just aren't spelled the way they sound, so a list of them is checked before the phonetic engine runs. A match there always wins over whatever the transliterator would have produced. Two kinds of word need this:

- **English loanwords.** "Keyboard" is written কিবোর্ড and "chair" চেয়ার by convention, not by sound. Left to the rules, "table" would come out তাব্লে instead of টেবিল. About 2,400 of these are listed by hand.
- **Banglish as people actually type it.** Chat spelling drops vowels wholesale, and no phonetic rule can put back a vowel that was never typed. `tmr` is তোমার, `amk` is আমাকে, `trpr` is তারপর. Colloquial and regional inflections like `boltiso` (বলতিসো) are in the same list, which covers about 12,000 spellings drawn from a corpus of real Bangla chat.

| you type | you get | without the list |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `tmr` | তোমার | ত্ম্র |
| `amk` | আমাকে | আম্ক |
| `tmi` | তুমি | ত্মি |
| `table` | টেবিল | তাব্লে |

A listed spelling shows up in the composing text as soon as you finish typing it, not when you hit space: `tmr` reads তোমার at the r. That's the same answer the space would have committed, so nothing changes under you at the last moment.

You can switch this off per language if you'd rather have the letter-for-letter reading (see **Spellings** under [Options](#options) below). See [Downloadable dictionaries](/languages/dictionaries/) for how Bangla's dictionary and these lists fit into suggestions generally.

## Choosing a layout

Bangla ships six layouts. A fresh install turns on one of them, Avro, and only if your phone's own language list has Bangla in it. What starts enabled is read off the phone rather than fixed (see [What ships](/languages/overview/#what-ships)). Turn the rest on whenever you want them, from Bangla's own screen under **Languages**. There's one exception: a keyboard you enabled from Android's settings and then typed on without ever opening the app. The setup wizard never ran, so the built-in fallback stands, which is Avro alongside English QWERTY.

- **Avro phonetic**: the QWERTY grid described above, and the default layout when you add Bangla by searching for it.
- **প্রভাত (Probhat)**: the fixed Bangla layout popular on Linux, following the standard desktop keymap as shipped by Avro Keyboard and OpenBangla. You type Bengali letters directly, with nothing transliterated.
- **জাতীয় (National / Jatiya)**: the Bangladesh national standard fixed layout, also following its desktop keymap.
- **বৈশাখী (Baishakhi)**: another fixed Bangla layout, typed directly with nothing transliterated.
- **বর্ণনা (Bornona)**: another fixed Bangla layout, typed directly with nothing transliterated.
- **ইনস্ক্রিপ্ট (InScript)**: the Bengali InScript layout, the same Indic-script keyboard standard the app ships for many other Indian languages.

</PhoneFrame>

On Probhat, shift mostly gives you the aspirated partner of a consonant (দ→ধ, ট→ঠ, গ→ঘ, ক→খ) but not uniformly. Some shift pairs are vowel-length pairs (ি→ই, ু→উ, ী→ঈ) and a few are unrelated letters that just share a key for space reasons (র→ড়, ন→ণ, স→ষ). The 10th key on the home row carries ে with ো on shift and ৈ/ৌ on long-press. A handful of less common marks (ৎ, ঞ, ঁ, ৃ, ৗ, ঽ) live as long-press alternates on a phonetically related key rather than getting a dedicated slot.

Jatiya's letter keys also match its desktop layout. Independent vowels that sit on a desktop AltGr key are reached here as a long-press on their matching kar sign: long-press ু for উ or ঊ, long-press ি for ই or ঈ, and so on.

All six layouts type দাঁড়ি (।) from the key next to the spacebar rather than a full stop, with the ASCII `.` moved to that key's long-press for numbers, file names and web addresses.

বিসর্গ (ঃ) sits on the long-press of the colon key on the symbols layer, on all six. It's drawn as a colon and does a different job, and the symbol layers are one grid shared by every language, so the colon's popup is where it goes.

All five fixed layouts share the same cluster-editing behavior as Avro (see [Editing conjuncts and clusters](#editing-conjuncts-and-clusters) below), and the same font choice applies across all six (see [Options](#options)). Switching between them works exactly like switching between any other enabled layouts: spacebar swipe, hold-drag picker, or the <KeyCap>🌐</KeyCap> key. See [Switching languages](/languages/switching/) for the full mechanics.

## Editing conjuncts and clusters

Bengali script builds conjuncts (যুক্তবর্ণ) by joining consonants with a hasant (্): ক্ষ, স্ত্রী, জ্ঞ. By default, backspace over already-typed Bengali text removes one character at a time, which can leave a half-formed cluster on screen: জ্ঞ with one backspace becomes জ্, not জ.

<SettingsPath path="Languages & layouts / Bangla / Clusters / Conjunct-aware backspace" />

Turning on **Conjunct-aware backspace** changes this: a whole conjunct cluster is deleted in a single press instead. স্ত্রী (a triple conjunct) disappears as one unit, ক্ষ as one unit, and a bare trailing hasant with nothing joined to it yet still deletes alone. The setting is **off by default**, and it applies to committed Bengali-script text across all six Bangla layouts alike, which share the same cluster logic. It only affects text already committed to the field. Deleting through your still-composing Avro romanization always removes one Latin character per press.

This toggle isn't unique to Bangla. Every language on a cluster-forming script gets its own copy of it, and Devanagari, Tamil, Khmer and others work the same way. It's set per language, so Bangla deleting whole conjuncts doesn't commit you to Hindi doing the same.

## Emoji search in Bangla

Bangla is one of the two languages (with English) whose search keywords are baked directly into the bundled emoji catalog, so searching emoji by their Bangla name works with nothing to download. A larger keyword pack for Bangla is downloadable too, and it stacks on top of the bundled set rather than replacing it. - **Layouts**: one card each for Avro, Probhat, Jatiya, Baishakhi, Bornona, and InScript, each a small preview of that keyboard. Tap a card to turn it on or off; all six are independent. At least one layout has to stay enabled somewhere across your whole keyboard, so you can't switch off the last one from here.
- **Key hints**: <SettingsPath path="Languages & layouts / Bangla / Key hints / Show the letter each key types" />, a three-way choice (Off, Added, or Whole letter), off by default. It puts the Bengali letter a key is about to write in its corner. Unlike the settings around it, this one is shared. It's one setting for every language with a transliterating layout enabled, not just Bangla, and it shows only while such a layout, Avro, is active.
- **Key map**: a folded group just below the hints while Avro is on. Open it for the vowels, consonants, joins and signs, the same tables as the [Avro key map](#avro-key-map) above, so they work offline. **Full guide** at the bottom, <SettingsPath path="Languages & layouts / Bangla / Key map / Full guide" />, opens this page in your browser.
- **Suggestion strip**: <SettingsPath path="Languages & layouts / Bangla / Suggestion strip / Keep the first two words in place" />, off by default, shown while Avro is on. Normally the first word on the strip is the one the space bar will write, so the words move as you type. Turn this on and the first two never move: on the left is what you typed, in English letters, and next to it is the Avro reading of it, letter for letter (`ami` → আমি, `tmr` → ত্ম্র). The suggestions come after them. The space bar still writes the same word as before, and that word is the bold one wherever it sits. A second row, **Suggestions after them**, picks what fills the rest of the strip: **Smart** (both languages, the one the word reads as first, with the other language's best word on the last chip when there is room), **Bengali only**, or **English only**. English only works without English under Also suggest from too, from the bundled English list. While this is on, the strip does not move its top word to the middle.
- **Numerals**: two buttons, **০-৯** and **0-9**, and Bengali digits win by default. Switch to `0-9` if you'd rather see Latin digits on the number row while typing Bangla. Where the digits you pick actually get typed is a separate, global setting under Layout & size → Numerals. It defaults to native digits in text fields only, and plain ASCII in number, phone, date and time fields.
- **Also suggest from**: pick other enabled languages to draw secondary suggestions from alongside Bangla. With English picked, Avro's strip also carries the word as you typed it in Latin letters, and an **English words** group appears right below: **Type English words as English** makes the space bar write `hello` as hello, and **Switch on the suggestion strip** shows or hides the small button that turns that on and off while you type. See [English on Avro and Hindi phonetic](/smart/mixed-language/#english-on-avro-and-hindi-phonetic).
- **Dictionary**: Bangla ships with a bundled dictionary, and a downloadable wordlist of about 451,000 words gives better suggestions. You can import your own list too. The downloaded list has no word frequencies of its own, so the bundled list's ranking is kept over it: without that, every sibling would tie and `sorasori` could commit a rarer word that shares its phonetic key instead of সরাসরি. See [Downloadable dictionaries](/languages/dictionaries/) for how sizes compare across languages.
- **Emoji**: download the larger Bangla emoji-keyword pack here, which names about 3,900 emoji. The bundled catalog's Bangla keywords already work without it. The pack arrives on its own if [Download automatically](/emoji/customization/) is on.

- **Spellings**: <SettingsPath path="Languages & layouts / Bangla / Spellings / Use known spellings" />, on by default. This is the list described above, and it's what makes `table` commit টেবিল rather than তাব্লে, and `tmr` commit তোমার rather than ত্ম্র. Switch it off if you want the letter-for-letter reading instead, which is otherwise unreachable because the list outranks every other suggestion source. The setting is per language, so turning it off for Bangla says nothing about any other script.
- **Dictionary words**: <SettingsPath path="Languages & layouts / Bangla / Spellings / Correct to dictionary words" />, on by default. This is the phonetic fold described above, the step that turns `asi` into আছি when you press space. It runs whether or not autocorrect is on, because it's part of how Avro typing reaches real words. Switch it off and space always commits exactly what the Avro rules spell (`asi` is আসি); the dictionary words stay in the suggestion strip for you to tap. Known spellings are separate, so `tmr` still gives তোমার unless you turn that off too.
- **Clusters**: <SettingsPath path="Languages & layouts / Bangla / Clusters / Conjunct-aware backspace" />, covered above, off by default.

One more setting for Bangla lives outside this screen:

- The **Bengali font** row, at <SettingsPath path="Appearance / Keyboard font / Bengali font" />, is one of the per-script font sections. It shows only while a Bengali-script language is enabled, and it's separate from the general keyboard font. Your choice applies to all six Bangla layouts at once. The section offers the automatic Noto face, eight Bengali-focused Google Fonts, anything in your font library that covers Bengali, and an import button for a `.ttf` or `.otf` of your own. Every script section takes an import, so this isn't Bengali's alone.

## Details & edge cases

**"Banglish" is a different language, not a mode of this one.** Selecting Banglish · Bangla (Romanized) from the language catalogue gives you a plain Latin QWERTY that stays in Latin script (it never converts to Bengali script) and pulls suggestions from its own romanized word list. It has its own emoji keyword pack too: the Bangla keywords in the Latin spellings people chat in (`hasi` and `hashi` both find 😀, `biral` finds the cats, `bhat` and `vat` find 🍚), with the English keywords alongside. That's for people who want to text in Bangla using Latin letters and keep it that way. Avro, covered on this page, is the opposite: it converts what you type into Bengali script. Don't confuse the two if you're looking for one and land on the other in search results.

**A few other languages share the Bengali script** but aren't the same language entry: Assamese, Bishnupriya Manipuri, and Sylheti all use Bengali-script layouts and Bengali-style numerals, but none of them bundle a dictionary the way Bangla does. If you're typing one of those, look for it by its own name in the language catalogue rather than under Bangla.

**The near-tie protection can feel inconsistent if you don't know it's there.** Because a phonetic sibling only overrides the literal transliteration when it's at least twice as frequent, two words that are close in frequency won't always resolve the way you expect. This is deliberate, so the keyboard never silently swaps in a different word than what the composing preview showed you a moment before you hit space. It only applies to spellings the fixed list doesn't already name, since a listed spelling settles the commit before frequency is consulted at all.

**The layout editor shows both shift labels at once for Probhat and Jatiya keys**, even though the real keyboard only shows one label per key state. Many fonts render a bare vowel sign (া, ি) as an orphaned, hard-to-read mark on its own, so the editor shows the base and shift labels side by side purely so you can tell keys apart while editing. This doesn't change what's drawn on the actual keyboard.

**A rearranged Bangla layout still types Bangla.** If you open Probhat, Jatiya, or Avro in the [layout editor](/languages/custom-layouts/) and move keys around, the result still draws suggestions from the Bangla dictionary and still composes Bengali script. A layout's language comes from what it's based on, not from its key arrangement.
